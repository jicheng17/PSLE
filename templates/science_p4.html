Ch 1Plant System

Systems · Plants

Parts of a plant

Key facts: parts and their functions

  • RootsHold the plant firmly in the soil. Take in water and mineral salts from the soil.
  • StemHolds the leaves and flowers up towards the light. Carries water and mineral salts from the roots to the other parts, and carries food made in the leaves to all parts.
  • LeavesMake food for the plant, using light, water and carbon dioxide from the air.
  • FlowersMake the seeds, so the plant can reproduce. A flower that is pollinated develops into a fruit.
  • FruitsProtect the seeds, and may help to spread them away from the parent plant.
  • SeedsCan grow into new plants.
Watch out: a plant makes its own food in the leaves; it does not "eat" the soil. The roots take in water and mineral salts, not food. Say "makes food", not "takes in food".
Your turn

Which part of the plant takes in water from the soil, and which part carries it up to the leaves?

The roots take in water; the stem carries it up to the leaves.

Potato, carrot and the parts we eat

  • Potato: a stem, not a rootA potato is an underground stem (a stem tuber) that stores food made by the leaves. Its "eyes" are buds that can grow into new shoots, which roots cannot do.
  • Carrot and sweet potato: rootsThese are roots that store food. A carrot is a tap root; a sweet potato is a swollen root.

Which part of the plant do we eat?

PartExamples
RootsCarrot, radish, sweet potato
StemsPotato, sugar cane

Watch out: where a plant part grows is not enough to name it. A potato grows underground like a root, but it is a stem. Look at what it does: stems have buds that grow shoots, roots do not.

The digestive system

Parts and their functions

  • DigestionThe process of breaking food down into tiny pieces that can be taken into the blood.
  • MouthTeeth cut, tear and grind food into small pieces. Saliva wets the food and starts to break it down. The tongue helps to mix and swallow it.
  • GulletA tube that pushes food down to the stomach. Its muscles squeeze the food along.
  • StomachMuscles churn the food while digestive juices break it down into a thick liquid.
  • Small intestineDigestion is finished here. The digested food passes through the wall into the blood.
  • Large intestineTakes water from the food that cannot be digested. What is left becomes solid waste (faeces).
  • AnusThe faeces leave the body through the anus.
Your turn

In which part of the digestive system does digested food pass into the blood?

The small intestine.

How the parts work together

  1. 1 · MouthFood is chewed into small pieces and mixed with saliva.
  2. 2 · GulletMuscles push the food down to the stomach.
  3. 3 · StomachFood is churned and mixed with digestive juices into a thick liquid.
  4. 4 · Small intestineDigestion is completed. Digested food passes into the blood.
  5. 5 · Large intestineWater is taken from the undigested food. The rest becomes faeces.
  6. 6 · AnusFaeces are pushed out of the body.

Key facts

  • Order of the food pathMouth → gullet → stomach → small intestine → large intestine → anus.
  • Where digestion happensIt starts in the mouth, carries on in the stomach, and is finished in the small intestine. The gullet only moves the food along, and the large intestine only takes water from what is left.
  • Working togetherEach part does one job and passes the food on to the next. If one part stops working, the whole system is affected.
  • Where digested food goesThe blood (circulatory system) carries it to all parts of the body. See the flow chart in Primary 5 Science.
Watch out: food does not go through the lungs, and air does not go through the stomach. The gullet is the food pipe; the windpipe is for air. Do not mix them up.

Ch 3Matter

Cycles · Matter

The three states of matter

Key facts

  • MatterAnything that has mass and takes up space (has volume). Solids, liquids and gases are all matter, including the air around us.
  • SolidHas a fixed shape and a fixed volume, e.g. a stone, a wooden block.
  • LiquidHas no fixed shape: it takes the shape of the container it is in. Has a fixed volume, e.g. water, milk.
  • GasHas no fixed shape and no fixed volume: it spreads out to fill the whole container, e.g. air, steam. Gases are often colourless and cannot be seen, but they have mass and take up space.
  • MassThe amount of matter in an object. Measured with a balance, in grams (g) or kilograms (kg).
  • VolumeThe amount of space that matter takes up. Units: cubic centimetres (cm³), millilitres (mL) or litres (L). 1 mL = 1 cm³.

How are mass and volume measured?

  • Mass of a solid or liquidUse a balance. For a liquid, find the mass of the container with the liquid, then subtract the mass of the empty container.
  • Volume of a liquidPour it into a measuring cylinder. Place the cylinder on a flat surface and read the scale with your eye level with the bottom of the curved surface.
  • Volume of an irregular solidPut it in water in a measuring cylinder (the solid must sink and not dissolve). Volume = final water level − starting water level.
  • Volume of a gasA gas fills its container, so its volume is the volume of the container.
Watch out: "mass" is not "volume". A big balloon of air takes up lots of space but has very little mass, and a small metal ball takes up little space but has more mass.
Your turn

A stone is placed in a measuring cylinder with 40 mL of water. The water level rises to 55 mL. What is the volume of the stone?

55 mL − 40 mL = 15 mL (15 cm³).

Ch 4Light

Energy · Light

How do we see?

Key facts: where does light come from?

  • Source of lightSomething that gives out its own light. Natural sources: the Sun, stars, fire, lightning, fireflies. Man-made sources: a lamp, a torch, a candle flame, a TV screen.
  • The SunThe main natural source of light for Earth.
  • Not a sourceThe Moon, a mirror and a book do not make their own light. We see them because they reflect light from a source.

Key facts: how light helps us see

  • Seeing a sourceLight from the source travels straight into our eyes.
  • Seeing other objectsLight from a source hits the object and is reflected (bounces off) into our eyes. This is why we cannot see in a completely dark room.
  • Light travels in straight linesThis is why we cannot see around a corner.
Watch out: the Moon looks bright but is not a source of light. It reflects light from the Sun. Our eyes do not send out light to see things either: the light has to come into our eyes.
Your turn

Explain how you are able to see a book on a table in a bright room.

Light from a source (e.g. a lamp) hits the book and is reflected into my eyes, so I see the book.

Ch 5Shadows

Energy · Light and shadows

How does the distance of the object change the shadow?

Key facts: how shadows form

  • ShadowA dark area that forms behind an object when the object blocks light.
  • You need three thingsA source of light, an object that blocks light, and a screen or surface for the shadow to fall on.
  • Light travels in straight linesLight cannot bend round the object, so there is a dark area behind it where light cannot reach.
  • OpaqueBlocks all the light, so it forms a dark shadow, e.g. wood, metal, cardboard.
  • TranslucentLets some light through, so it forms a light shadow, e.g. tracing paper, frosted glass.
  • TransparentLets almost all light through, so it forms hardly any shadow, e.g. clear glass, clear plastic.

What changes the size and shape of a shadow?

  • Distance from the light sourceThe closer the object is to the light source, the bigger its shadow. The farther away, the smaller the shadow.
  • Position of the light sourceA light source high above gives a short shadow; a low one gives a long shadow. The shadow falls on the opposite side to the light source.
  • Shape and angle of the objectThe shadow has the same outline as the object as seen from the light. Turn the object and the shape of the shadow changes.
Watch out: a shadow can be much bigger than the object. Moving the object closer to the light source makes its shadow bigger. Say "closer to the light source", not "closer to the screen".

Ch 6Heat

Energy · Heat and temperature

A laboratory thermometer

Reading: 60 °C
Read the scale with your eye level with the top of the liquid.

Key facts

  • TemperatureHow hot or cold something is. Measured with a thermometer in degrees Celsius (°C).
  • HeatA form of energy. Heat flows from a hotter object to a colder one.
  • Heat gainWhen an object gains heat, its temperature rises.
  • Heat lossWhen an object loses heat, its temperature falls.
  • Using a thermometerThe bulb must be in the substance and not touch the sides or bottom of the container. Wait for the liquid to stop moving before reading. Read the scale at eye level.
  • Touch is not reliableOur hands only tell us how hot or cold something feels, not its exact temperature. Use a thermometer.

Temperature or heat?

  • TemperatureHow hot or cold something is. Shown by a thermometer reading in °C, e.g. the water is 80 °C.
  • HeatA form of energy that flows. Heat gain makes the temperature rise and heat loss makes it fall, e.g. a hot drink loses heat to the air, so it cools down.

What causes temperature to change?

  • Heat gainThe object is heated by a hotter object or heat source, e.g. a pot on a stove. Its temperature rises.
  • Heat lossThe object loses heat to colder surroundings, e.g. a cup of tea left on the table. Its temperature falls until it matches its surroundings.
Watch out: do not say "the cold goes into the object". Cold is not something that flows. The object loses heat, and its temperature falls.
Your turn

What is the difference between heat and temperature?

Temperature tells us how hot or cold something is and is measured with a thermometer. Heat is a form of energy that flows from a hotter object to a colder one.

Ch 7Effects of Heat

Energy · Heat flow

How does heat flow?

Key facts: heat flow and sources

  • Direction of heat flowFrom a hotter object or place to a colder one. It keeps flowing until both are at the same temperature.
  • Sources of heatThe Sun (the main natural source), burning fuel (fire, a candle, gas), electricity (a heater, an iron, a toaster), and rubbing things together (friction, e.g. rubbing your hands).
  • Good conductors of heatLet heat pass through quickly. Metals such as copper, aluminium and iron.
  • Poor conductors (insulators)Let heat pass through slowly. Wood, plastic, rubber, cloth, polystyrene and air.
  • UsesCooking pots are metal so heat reaches the food quickly, with plastic or wooden handles so we can hold them. A jacket or a thermos flask is made of insulators to slow heat loss.

Conductors and insulators

Heat flows...Materials
QuicklyCopper, aluminium, iron, steel (metals)
SlowlyWood, plastic, rubber, cloth, polystyrene, air
Watch out: a metal spoon feels colder than a wooden one at room temperature because metal conducts heat away from your hand faster. They are at the same temperature. Say "heat flows away from the hand faster".
Your turn

A metal spoon and a plastic spoon are left in a cup of hot soup. After a minute, which handle feels hotter and why?

The metal spoon, because metal is a good conductor of heat, so heat flows along it quickly. Plastic is a poor conductor.

  • Water in the cycleMelting, evaporation, condensation and freezing are the changes of state of water. See Cycles in Water in Primary 5 Science.

Exam habits for P4 science

  • Name the part, then its job"Roots take in water and mineral salts." Pair each part with its function.
  • Light must reach the eyeTo see anything, light must go into the eye, either straight from a source or reflected from an object.
  • Say heat gain or heat lossAvoid "cold goes in". Temperature changes because heat is gained or lost.
  • Compare with a reason"Metal is a good conductor, so heat flows quickly" earns more than "metal is hot".
  • Read scales carefullyEye level, and check what each small division is worth before reading.
  • Mass is not volumeMass is the amount of matter (g, kg). Volume is the space it takes up (mL, cm³).
