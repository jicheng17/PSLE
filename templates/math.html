Angles

Geometry

Angle facts at a glance

Key rules

  • Angles on a straight line = 180°A half turn.
  • Angles at a point = 360°A full turn.
  • Vertically opposite angles are equalWhere two straight lines cross.
  • Angles in a triangle = 180°Every triangle, any shape.
  • Isosceles: base angles equal. Equilateral: all 60°.Mark the equal sides first, then the equal angles.
  • Parallelogram, rhombus: opposite angles equal; neighbours add to 180°Learn these — questions won't remind you.

Worked example: angles at a point

Three angles meet at a point: 130°, 95° and x. Find x.

  • x = 360° − 130° − 95°
  • = 135°
135°

Worked example: isosceles triangle

An isosceles triangle has an apex angle of 52°. Find each base angle.

  • Two base angles = 180° − 52° = 128°
  • One base angle = 128° ÷ 2 = 64°
64°

Worked example: parallelogram

In parallelogram PQRS, angle P is 72°. Find angle Q.

  • P and Q are neighbours (they share a side)
  • Q = 180° − 72° = 108°
108°
Watch out: the question usually won't tell you a triangle is isosceles or a shape is a rhombus — you're expected to spot it from tick marks or the name given. Learn the shape properties by heart.
Your turn

Angles a, 50° and 75° sit together on a straight line. Find a.

a = 180° − 50° − 75° = 55°

Area

Measurement

Four formulas, one picture each

The height is always perpendicular to the base

Key rules

  • Rectangle: Area = length × breadthThe one you already know — the other formulas all build on it.
  • Triangle: Area = ½ × base × heightA triangle is always half of the rectangle with the same base and height.
  • Parallelogram: Area = base × heightThe height is the perpendicular distance between the two parallel sides, not the slanted side.
  • Trapezium: Area = ½ × (a + b) × heighta and b are the lengths of the two parallel sides.
  • Composite figure: split it, or complete it then subtractBreak the shape into rectangles and triangles you already know how to find.
  • Working backwards: rearrange the formulaE.g. base = 2 × area ÷ height for a triangle — undo the ½ by doubling first.

Worked example: area of a rectangle

A rectangle measures 15 cm by 8 cm. Find its area.

  • 15 × 8 = 120
120 cm²

Worked example: area of a triangle

A triangle has a base of 14 cm and a height of 9 cm. Find its area.

  • ½ × 14 × 9 = 63
63 cm²

Worked example: composite figure

A figure is made of a 10 cm by 6 cm rectangle with a right triangle of base 10 cm and height 4 cm attached to one side. Find the total area.

  • Rectangle = 10 × 6 = 60 cm²
  • Triangle = ½ × 10 × 4 = 20 cm²
  • 60 + 20 = 80
80 cm²

Worked example: missing height

A triangle has an area of 36 cm² and a base of 8 cm. Find its height.

  • Height = 2 × 36 ÷ 8
  • = 72 ÷ 8 = 9
9 cm

Worked example: area of a parallelogram

A parallelogram has a base of 16 cm and a height of 9 cm. Its slanted side is 11 cm. Find its area.

  • The slanted side (11 cm) is not the height — ignore it
  • 16 × 9 = 144
144 cm²

Worked example: area of a trapezium

A trapezium has parallel sides of 12 cm and 8 cm, and a height of 5 cm. Find its area.

  • ½ × (12 + 8) × 5
  • = ½ × 20 × 5 = 50
50 cm²
Watch out: for triangles, parallelograms, and trapeziums, never use a slanted side as the height — look for the small square that marks the right angle, or check the height is drawn straight up from the base (even if that means it falls outside the shape). For a trapezium, make sure a and b are the two parallel sides, not a slanted one.
Your turn

A parallelogram has a base of 9 cm and a height of 6 cm. Find its area.

9 × 6 = 54 cm²

Volume of cubes and cuboids

Measurement

A box packed with unit cubes

Key rules

  • Cuboid: V = l × b × hLength × breadth × height.
  • Cube: V = s × s × sAll edges are equal.
  • 1 L = 1 000 mL = 1 000 cm³Divide cm³ by 1 000 to get litres.
  • Missing edge: h = V ÷ (l × b)Divide the volume by the two edges you know.
  • Number of small cubes = big volume ÷ small volumeOr count how many fit along each edge and multiply.

Worked example: finding volume

A box is 12 cm long, 8 cm wide and 5 cm tall. Find its volume.

  • V = 12 × 8 × 5 = 480
480 cm³

Worked example: missing height

A cuboid has a volume of 252 cm³. Its length is 9 cm and its breadth is 7 cm. Find its height.

  • h = 252 ÷ (9 × 7)
  • = 252 ÷ 63 = 4
4 cm

Worked example: packing small cubes

A large box measures 40 cm by 30 cm by 20 cm. How many small cubes of edge 10 cm can exactly fill it?

  • Large box volume = 40 × 30 × 20 = 24 000 cm³
  • Small cube volume = 10 × 10 × 10 = 1 000 cm³
  • 24 000 ÷ 1 000 = 24
24 small cubes
Watch out: volume is measured in cubic units (cm³), never plain cm. When counting how many small cubes fit, make sure the small cube's edge divides exactly into each of the box's three edges — draw it out if you're not sure.
Your turn

A cube has edges of 6 cm. Find its volume, and say how many mL of water it can hold.

6 × 6 × 6 = 216 cm³ = 216 mL

Fractions

Number

"Of" a quantity means multiply

Key rules

  • Adding or subtracting: use a common denominator firstConvert both fractions before you add or subtract.
  • Mixed numbers: add/subtract whole parts and fraction partsRegroup if the fraction part doesn't have enough to subtract.
  • Fraction × whole number: multiply the numerator only3/5 × 20 = (3 × 20) ÷ 5
  • Fraction × fraction: multiply straight acrossNumerator × numerator, denominator × denominator, then simplify.
  • Mixed number × whole number: convert to an improper fraction firstThen multiply as usual.

Worked example: adding mixed numbers

Find 2 1/4 + 1 5/6.

  • Common denominator is 12: 2 3/12 + 1 10/12
  • = 3 13/12
  • = 4 1/12
4 1/12

Worked example: fraction of a quantity

There are 20 sweets in a bag. 3/5 of them are red. How many are red?

  • 3/5 × 20 = (3 × 20) ÷ 5
  • = 60 ÷ 5 = 12
12 sweets

Worked example: multiplying two fractions

Find 2/3 × 3/8.

  • (2 × 3) ÷ (3 × 8) = 6/24
  • = 1/4
1/4
Watch out: you can only add or subtract fractions once they share a denominator — never add numerators and denominators separately. When multiplying, look for numbers that cancel diagonally first; it keeps the numbers small.
Your turn

Find 3/4 × 2/9.

(3 × 2) ÷ (4 × 9) = 6/36 = 1/6

Decimals

Number

Key rules

  • × 10, 100, 1 000: move the point right1, 2 or 3 places. 0.6 × 100 = 60
  • ÷ 10, 100, 1 000: move the point left4.5 ÷ 100 = 0.045
  • Multiplying by a whole number: multiply normally, then place the pointCount the decimal places in the number you started with.
  • Dividing by a whole number: divide normally, point stays lined upSame place value, straight down.
  • Money: 2 decimal placesThe nearest cent.

Worked example: multiplying decimals

A pencil costs $0.65. Find the cost of 6 pencils.

  • 0.65 × 6 = 3.90
$3.90

Worked example: dividing decimals

A ribbon 9.6 m long is cut equally into 4 pieces. Find the length of each piece.

  • 9.6 ÷ 4 = 2.4
2.4 m

Worked example: × 100 shortcut

A jug holds 1.25 L of juice. Find the total volume of 100 identical jugs, in litres.

  • 1.25 × 100 = 125
125 L
Watch out: "0.6" and "0.06" are not the same size — line up the decimal point carefully when you write your working. When multiplying two decimals, count the total decimal places in both numbers; when multiplying a decimal by a whole number, only the decimal counts.
Your turn

Apple juice costs $1.85 per bottle. Find the cost of 8 bottles.

1.85 × 8 = $14.80

Rate

Number

Key rules

  • Rate = total ÷ time (or ÷ quantity)An amount for every one unit — L/min, $/kg, pages/min.
  • Total = rate × timeMultiply the rate by however many units you have.
  • Time = total ÷ rateUndo the multiplication.
  • Better buy: compare the cost for the same unitFind the cost per kg (or per item) for each option.

Worked example: basic rate

A tap fills a tank at a rate of 8 litres per minute. How many litres flow in 15 minutes?

  • 8 × 15 = 120
120 L

Worked example: finding the rate, then the time

A printer prints 45 pages in 5 minutes. Find its rate, then find how long it takes to print 180 pages.

  • Rate = 45 ÷ 5 = 9 pages per minute
  • Time = 180 ÷ 9 = 20
9 pages/min; 20 minutes

Worked example: better buy

A 2 kg bag of rice costs $5.60. A 5 kg bag costs $13.50. Which is the better buy?

  • 2 kg bag: 5.60 ÷ 2 = $2.80 per kg
  • 5 kg bag: 13.50 ÷ 5 = $2.70 per kg
The 5 kg bag
Watch out: identify what one "unit" of the rate actually is — per minute, per kg, per item — before you multiply or divide. Keep your answer's unit the same as the rate's unit.
Your turn

A machine packs 240 boxes in 6 hours. Find its rate, then find how long it takes to pack 600 boxes.

Rate = 240 ÷ 6 = 40 boxes/hour. Time = 600 ÷ 40 = 15 hours

Percentage

Number

Key rules

  • Part = percentage/100 × wholeFinding a percentage of a quantity.
  • Percentage = part/whole × 100%Expressing one quantity as a percentage of another.
  • Whole = part ÷ percentage × 100Working backwards from a part to the whole.
  • 100% is always the whole thingBe sure what the question calls "the whole" before you start.

Worked example: percentage of a quantity

Find 40% of 350.

  • 40/100 × 350 = 140
140

Worked example: expressing as a percentage

Ravi answered 18 out of 24 questions correctly. What percentage is that?

  • 18/24 × 100% = 75%
75%

Worked example: finding the whole

45 is 30% of a number. Find the number.

  • Whole = 45 ÷ 30 × 100
  • = 150
150
Watch out: "part/whole × 100%" and "percentage/100 × whole" are the same rule read in opposite directions — write down which one the question is asking for before calculating, not after.
Your turn

36 is 20% of a number. Find the number.

36 ÷ 20 × 100 = 180

Exam habits for P5 topics

  • Mark the figureFor angles, write every angle you find straight onto the diagram, with the reason beside it.
  • Name the wholeFor percentage and rate questions, be sure what "the whole" or "one unit" refers to.
  • Keep units consistentcm³ to litres, minutes to hours — convert before you combine numbers.
  • Line up decimal pointsFor addition and subtraction; for multiplication, count decimal places instead.
  • Show every stepMethod marks are given even if the final answer slips.
Contexts are Singapore-flavoured and distances are approximate. Numbers in examples are for practice, not real records.
