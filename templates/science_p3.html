Ch 1Diversity of Living and Non-living Things

Diversity · Living things

Living or non-living?

Living thingsNon-living things
ExamplesPlants, animals, humans, mushroomsRocks, water, a car, a toy, a pencil
Need food, water and airYesNo
GrowYesNo (they may change size, but not by growing)
ReproduceYesNo
Respond to changesYesNo

Key facts

  • ObservingWe use our senses (sight, hearing, smell, touch, taste) to observe things around us. Never taste or smell unknown things; they may be harmful.
  • What living things needFood, water and air. Plants make their own food (using light, water and air). Animals get food by eating plants or other animals.
  • What living things can doGrow, reproduce (make young like themselves), respond to changes around them, and move (animals move from place to place; plants move only parts, e.g. leaves turning to the light).
  • Non-living thingsDo not need food, water or air, and cannot grow, reproduce or respond.
Watch out: moving is not enough to be alive. A car, a river and clouds all move but they are non-living. And a plant that does not seem to move is alive: it grows, makes food and responds to light.
Your turn

A toy robot walks and talks. Is it living? Give a reason.

No. It does not need food, water or air, and it cannot grow or reproduce.

Ch 2Classification of Living Things

Diversity · Groups of living things

Groups of living things

  • Living things
    • Plants
      • Flowering plantsrose, hibiscus, mango tree
      • Non-flowering plantsferns, mosses
    • Animals
      • With a backbonefish, frog, lizard, bird, cat
      • Without a backbonebutterfly, spider, snail, worm
    • Fungimushroom, mould, yeast
    • Bacteriatoo small to see without a microscope

Plants and animals: similar and different

  • BothNeed food, water and air; grow; reproduce; respond to changes.
  • PlantsMake their own food. Usually cannot move from place to place. Have roots, stems and leaves.
  • AnimalsCannot make their own food, so they eat plants or other animals. Most can move from place to place.

Other groups

  • FungiCannot make their own food. They take food from other living or dead things. Mushrooms, mould and yeast are fungi.
  • BacteriaVery tiny living things; we need a microscope to see them. Some are harmful and cause illness. Some are helpful, e.g. used to make yoghurt.
Watch out: a mushroom looks like a plant but it is a fungus. It does not make its own food. Use how a living thing gets its food, not just how it looks.
Your turn

Name one way plants and animals are different.

Plants make their own food, but animals cannot; animals must eat other living things.

Ch 3Diversity of Materials

Diversity · Materials

Does light pass through the material?

Key facts

  • MaterialsWhat objects are made of. Common materials: plastic, metal, wood, glass, rubber, fabric (cloth), paper, ceramic.
  • Physical propertiesHow a material looks, feels or behaves. We choose a material for an object by its properties.
  • StrengthA strong material does not break easily when pushed or pulled, e.g. steel. A weak one breaks easily, e.g. paper.
  • FlexibilityA flexible material bends easily without breaking, e.g. rubber, cloth. A stiff one does not, e.g. wood.
  • Waterproof or absorbentA waterproof material does not let water through, e.g. plastic. An absorbent material soaks water up, e.g. cloth, paper towel.
  • Float or sinkSome materials float on water, e.g. wood. Others sink, e.g. metal coin.
  • TransparencyTransparent, translucent or opaque (see the diagrams above).

How are different materials used?

MaterialPropertyUsed for
GlassTransparentWindows, spectacles
RubberFlexible, waterproofTyres, boots
PlasticWaterproof, lightRaincoats, bottles
MetalStrong, hardBridges, pots, tools
ClothFlexible, absorbentClothes, towels
WoodStrong, stiffFurniture
Watch out: give the property, then the use. "Rubber is used for boots because it is waterproof" is a full answer; "rubber is used for boots" is not.
Your turn

Why is a window made of glass and not wood?

Glass is transparent, so light passes through and we can see through it. Wood is opaque.

Ch 4Life Cycles of Plants

Cycles · Plants

Life cycle of a plant

Key facts

  • Life cycleThe series of stages in the life of a living thing, from the start of its life until it can make young like itself, which starts the cycle again.
  • SeedA seed can grow into a new plant. Many seeds are made in fruits.
  • GerminationWhen a seed starts to grow. It needs water, air and warmth (it does not need light or soil to start).
  • SeedlingThe tiny young plant that comes out of the seed: a root grows down and a shoot grows up.
  • Young plantThe seedling grows taller and grows more leaves.
  • Adult plantMakes flowers. The flowers make fruits that hold new seeds, so the cycle starts again.
Watch out: a seed needs water, air and warmth to germinate, but not sunlight. Light is needed later, once the leaves can make food.
Your turn

List the stages in the life cycle of a plant in order.

Seed → seedling → young plant → adult plant (which makes flowers, fruits and new seeds).

Ch 5Life Cycles of Animals

Cycles · Animals

Four stages or three stages

Life cycles of different animals

AnimalStages
ButterflyEgg → larva → pupa → adult
MosquitoEgg → larva → pupa → adult (the first three stages live in water)
CockroachEgg → nymph → adult
FrogEgg → tadpole → froglet → adult frog
ChickenEgg → chick → adult
Cat, dog, humanBaby (born alive) → young → adult

Similar or different?

  • SimilarEvery animal starts as an egg or a baby, grows, becomes an adult, and the adult makes young so the cycle repeats.
  • Different: number of stagesSome have 4 stages (butterfly, mosquito, frog), some have 3 (cockroach, chicken), and mammals have no egg stage.
  • Different: look of the youngA caterpillar or tadpole looks very different from its adult. A nymph, chick or kitten looks like a smaller adult.
  • Different: where it startsMost insects, birds and frogs lay eggs. Mammals such as cats and humans give birth to live young.
Your turn

Name one way the life cycle of a butterfly is different from that of a cockroach.

A butterfly has 4 stages, including a pupa, but a cockroach has only 3 stages (egg, nymph, adult). A caterpillar looks very different from the adult, but a nymph looks like a small adult.

Ch 6Properties of Magnets

Interactions · Magnets

Poles, pull and direction

Key facts

  • MagnetAn object that attracts (pulls) certain materials and can repel (push away) or attract other magnets.
  • Magnetic materialsIron, steel, nickel and cobalt are attracted to a magnet. Examples: paper clips, nails, steel cans.
  • Non-magnetic materialsPlastic, wood, glass, paper, cloth, rubber, and metals like copper and aluminium are not attracted.
  • PolesEvery magnet has two poles: a north pole (N) and a south pole (S). The pull is strongest at the poles and weakest in the middle.

Magnets together and on their own

  • Unlike poles attractA north pole and a south pole pull towards each other.
  • Like poles repelTwo north poles, or two south poles, push each other away.
  • Freely suspended magnetA magnet that can swing freely always settles pointing north–south, with its north pole pointing north. This is how a compass works.
Watch out: not all metals are magnetic. Copper and aluminium are metals but a magnet does not attract them. Only iron, steel, nickel and cobalt do.
Your turn

Two magnets are pushed together and they push apart. What does this tell you about the poles that were facing each other?

They were like poles (north–north or south–south), because like poles repel.

Ch 7Making and Using Magnets

Interactions · Magnets

Making a magnet by stroking

How can we make a magnet?

  • What to useA bar of a magnetic material such as iron or steel, and a magnet.
  • MethodStroke the bar from one end to the other with one pole of the magnet. Always stroke in the same direction. Lift the magnet well away from the bar and bring it back to the start. Repeat many times.
  • Test itThe bar is now a magnet if it can attract paper clips or other magnetic objects.
  • Keep magnets strongMagnets can lose their strength if they are dropped, hit or heated, so handle them with care.

How are magnets used in everyday life?

  • Fridge magnetsHold notes and pictures on a steel fridge door.
  • Door catchesA magnet in the door or cupboard holds it shut.
  • Pencil cases and bag flapsMagnetic fasteners keep them closed without a zip.
  • CompassesA small magnetic needle points north, to show direction.
  • Scrap yards and recyclingLarge magnets pick up iron and steel and separate them from other rubbish.
  • Toys and gamesMagnetic toys, building sets and boards.
Watch out: to separate a mixture, say which material the magnet picks up and why: "a magnet attracts the steel nails because steel is a magnetic material". It will not pick up plastic or aluminium.
Your turn

A pile of paper clips (steel), plastic beads and wooden sticks are mixed up. How can you quickly pick out the paper clips?

Move a magnet over the mixture. It attracts the steel paper clips only, because steel is a magnetic material. Plastic and wood are not.

Exam habits for P3 science

  • Give a reasonSay "because…". "It is living" is not enough; "it grows and needs food" is.
  • Use the right word for the stageSeedling, larva, pupa, nymph, tadpole: each belongs to one particular life cycle.
  • Property, then useName the property that makes a material suitable, then say what it is used for.
  • Poles, not endsSay "like poles repel" and "unlike poles attract". Say which poles faced each other.
  • Magnetic is not the same as metalOnly iron, steel, nickel and cobalt are attracted by a magnet.
  • Observe, don't guessUse your senses safely and write what you saw, not what you expected.
