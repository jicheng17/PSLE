Ch 1Reproduction in Animals and Plants

Cycles · Plants

Parts of a flower

Key facts

  • PollinationTransfer of pollen grains from the anther to the stigma. Self-pollination: within the same flower or plant. Cross-pollination: to a flower on a different plant of the same species.
  • Two main agentsWind and insects carry pollen between flowers.
  • Wind-pollinated flowersSmall, dull, no scent or nectar; produce lots of light, powdery pollen; feathery stigma to catch it.
  • Insect-pollinated flowersLarge, brightly coloured, scented, with nectar; pollen is larger and sticky or spiky so it clings to insects.
  • FertilisationA pollen grain fuses with an ovule inside the ovary, forming a zygote that develops into a seed. The ovary grows into the fruit.
  • Why seeds are dispersedTo avoid overcrowding and competition for light, water and nutrients, and to colonise new areas.
Watch out: pollination and fertilisation are not the same event. Pollination just lands the pollen on the stigma; fertilisation is the pollen fusing with the ovule afterwards. A flower can be pollinated and still fail to be fertilised.

Ch 1Reproduction in Animals and Plants

Cycles · Animals and humans

Three kinds of life cycle

Arrows run clockwise from stage to stage.

Key facts

  • No metamorphosisThe young resembles the adult from birth or hatching and simply grows bigger. No larva or pupa stage.
  • Incomplete metamorphosisEgg → nymph → adult. The nymph looks like a small, wingless version of the adult, and moults (sheds its skin) as it grows.
  • Complete metamorphosisEgg → larva → pupa → adult. The larva looks nothing like the adult, and the pupa is a resting stage where the body is rebuilt.
  • Human reproductionA sperm (from the testes) fuses with an egg (from the ovaries) in the oviduct — this is fertilisation, forming a zygote.
  • Growth before birthThe zygote implants in the uterus and develops into an embryo, then a fetus, fed through the placenta and umbilical cord for about 9 months before birth.
Watch out: a cockroach nymph already looks a lot like the adult, but it still moults and has no wings yet — that makes it incomplete metamorphosis, not "no metamorphosis". Only animals that look the same at every stage (like humans, dogs, chickens) count as no metamorphosis.
Your turn

A mosquito's life cycle goes egg → larva → pupa → adult. What type of life cycle is this?

Complete metamorphosis — four very different-looking stages, including a pupa.

Ch 2Cycles in Water

Cycles · States of matter and the water cycle

Changing state

Key facts

  • SolidFixed shape and volume. Particles are tightly packed and only vibrate in place.
  • LiquidFixed volume but takes the shape of its container. Particles are close but slide past each other.
  • GasNo fixed shape or volume; spreads to fill its container. Particles are far apart and move quickly.
  • Evaporation vs boilingEvaporation happens at the surface, at any temperature. Boiling happens throughout the liquid, only at its boiling point.
  • Water cycle stagesEvaporation → condensation → precipitation → collection, then it repeats.

The water cycle

Your turn

Puddles shrink and disappear on a hot, sunny day. Which change of state is this, and why?

Evaporation — the sun heats the surface of the water and turns it into water vapour (a gas).

Ch 4The Human Respiratory and Circulatory Systems

Systems · Respiratory

Path of air

Key facts

  • Path of air inNose → windpipe (trachea) → bronchus → bronchioles → alveoli (tiny air sacs) in the lungs.
  • Bronchus (plural: bronchi)The windpipe splits into two bronchi, one going to each lung. Each bronchus is a tube that carries air into and out of its lung, then branches into smaller and smaller tubes (bronchioles).
  • Job of the bronchi and windpipeRings of cartilage hold the tubes open so air can always flow. Mucus and tiny hairs lining them trap dust and germs that got past the nose and sweep them up and out.
  • BronchiolesThe thinner tubes inside the lung. They spread the air out to millions of alveoli, so every part of the lung gets fresh air.
  • Job of the noseTiny hairs and mucus filter out dust and germs, and warm and moisten the air.
  • Gas exchangeIn the alveoli, oxygen passes into the blood and carbon dioxide passes out, across thin walls surrounded by capillaries.
  • Breathing inThe diaphragm contracts and moves down; the chest cavity gets bigger; air is drawn into the lungs.
  • Breathing outThe diaphragm relaxes and moves up; the chest cavity gets smaller; air is pushed out.
Watch out: "breathing" (the physical movement of air in and out) is not the same as "respiration" (the chemical process inside cells that releases energy from food using oxygen). Use the term the question asks for.
Your turn

Where exactly does gas exchange take place, and why are its walls so thin?

In the alveoli. Thin walls let oxygen and carbon dioxide pass through quickly and easily.

What is the job of a bronchus, and how many are there?

It carries air between the windpipe and a lung. There are two, one for each lung.

Ch 4The Human Respiratory and Circulatory Systems

Systems · Circulatory

Heart and blood vessels

Key facts

  • Job of the heartPumps blood around the body through blood vessels, carrying oxygen, nutrients, carbon dioxide and waste.
  • Four chambersTwo atria (upper) receive blood; two ventricles (lower) pump blood out.
  • ArteriesCarry blood away from the heart. Thick, elastic walls to handle high pressure.
  • VeinsCarry blood back to the heart. Thinner walls, with valves to stop blood flowing backward.
  • CapillariesTiny vessels linking arteries to veins. Thin walls let oxygen, nutrients, carbon dioxide and waste pass through to and from cells.
  • BloodRed blood cells carry oxygen; white blood cells fight infection; platelets help blood clot; plasma carries it all.
Watch out: "artery = away from the heart" and "vein = towards the heart" is always true by direction — but not every artery carries oxygen-rich blood. The artery to the lungs carries oxygen-poor blood, and the vein from the lungs carries oxygen-rich blood.
Your turn

Which blood vessel has valves, and what are the valves for?

Veins. The valves stop blood from flowing backward as it travels toward the heart.

Ch 4Humans vs fish: breathing and circulation

Systems · Extension (not in the textbook contents)

Breathing: gills take oxygen from water

Circulation: one loop vs two

Key facts

  • Fish breathe with gillsGills take oxygen that is dissolved in water. Water goes in through the mouth, over the gills, and out under the gill covers.
  • Why gills work wellMany thin filaments (large surface area), very thin walls, and blood vessels close to the surface — the same reasons alveoli work in human lungs.
  • Opposite flowBlood flows the opposite way to the water, so the blood keeps meeting water with more oxygen in it.
  • Fish heart: 2 chambersOne atrium and one ventricle. It pumps blood to the gills.
  • Single circulation (fish)Heart → gills → body → heart. Blood passes through the heart once per full loop.
  • Double circulation (humans)Heart → lungs → heart → body → heart. Blood passes through the heart twice per full trip, so it is pushed on again after the lungs.
  • Why the differenceBlood pressure drops after the tiny gill vessels, so blood reaches a fish's body more slowly. That is enough for a cold-blooded animal that needs less energy.
Watch out: fish gills and single circulation are not in the Primary 5 textbook contents, so treat this as extension. If a question asks about gas exchange, the exam reasons are the same everywhere: thin walls, many blood vessels, large surface area. Don't say fish "breathe water" — they take dissolved oxygen out of it.
Your turn

How many times does blood pass through a fish's heart on one full loop around the body? How is that different in humans?

Once (heart → gills → body → heart). In humans it passes through the heart twice: once to go to the lungs and once to go to the body.

Ch 5Electrical Systems

Systems · includes series circuits from Ch 6

Closed circuit vs open circuit

Key facts

  • What a circuit needsA power source (battery), conductors (wires), and a load (bulb, buzzer) all forming one complete, unbroken loop.
  • SwitchClosed switch completes the circuit, so current flows and the bulb lights. Open switch breaks it, so the bulb stays off.
  • ConductorsLet electric current pass through easily, e.g. copper, iron and other metals.
  • InsulatorsBlock electric current, e.g. plastic, rubber, wood, glass. Used to cover wires safely.
  • Series circuitAll parts share a single loop. If one bulb blows or a wire breaks, the whole circuit stops — there's no other path for current.
  • More bulbs, dimmer lightBulbs added in series to the same battery share the same current, so each one glows more dimly.
Watch out: the exam-friendly rule is simple — a circuit must be one complete, unbroken loop. A single gap anywhere (an open switch, a missing wire, a blown bulb in series) stops current everywhere in that loop, not just at the gap.
Your turn

Three bulbs are connected in series. One bulb blows. What happens to the other two, and why?

They go off too. In a series circuit there is only one path, so a break anywhere stops the current everywhere.

Exam habits for science

  • Use the exact term"Respire" is not "breathe"; "melt" is not "boil". Precise words earn marks.
  • Label diagrams neatlyUse a ruler for straight lines, and write labels clearly.
  • Give a reasonAnswer "because…" or "so that…", not just what happens.
  • Compare both waysWhen asked to compare, give a similarity and a difference.
  • Watch the direction word"Cools" points to condensation or freezing; "heated" points to evaporation, boiling or melting.
  • Trace the whole loopFor circuits, check every part of the path is actually connected.
